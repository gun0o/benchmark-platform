# M2.2 — Statistics

*Milestone notes, written for someone learning benchmark methodology. Measured on
2026-09-18 on a Core Ultra 9 185H laptop under WSL2, release build, plugged in. Raw runs
and commands are in `docs/results/m2.2/`. Earlier notes: `M1.2.md`, `M2.1.md`.*

## What was built

The engine could already produce a thousand honest numbers. It could not yet say anything
*about* them. This milestone adds the summary layer: after each configuration finishes, the
runner reports how the trials were distributed, and the whole run gains a safety cap so an
overnight sweep cannot run forever.

1. **A statistics library** ([stats.hpp](../../engine/include/bench/stats.hpp),
   [stats.cpp](../../engine/src/stats.cpp)): `mean`, `sample_variance`, `sample_stddev`,
   `percentile`, `median`, `cov`, `mad`, a `Welford` accumulator with a merge operation,
   and `summarize()` which produces all of them in one pass over a sorted copy.
2. **A `summary` entry per configuration** in the run envelope, holding
   `n, mean, median, stddev, cov, min, p5, p95, max, mad` next to the per-trial `results`.
   Warmup trials are excluded, exactly as they are excluded from `results`.
3. **`--max-seconds`**, a cap on the whole run. It is checked at trial boundaries, so a
   capped run still ends cleanly, still writes valid JSON, and still summarizes the trials
   it managed to measure.
4. **Reference fixtures** ([stats.json](../../engine/tests/fixtures/stats.json), generated by
   [gen_stats_fixture.py](../../engine/tests/fixtures/gen_stats_fixture.py)): ten arrays and
   their statistics computed by numpy, committed so the C++ must agree to a relative
   tolerance of 1e-9.
5. **Summary validation**: `bench validate` now checks summary entries the way it checks
   results, including invariants a correct summary cannot violate (min ≤ median ≤ max,
   p5 ≤ p95, non-negative stddev, metric matching workload).

## Each correctness decision, and why

### Why the definitions are pinned to numpy

"Standard deviation" and "95th percentile" both have more than one answer, and the
differences are big enough to matter when the target is a coefficient of variation of 3%.

**Sample versus population standard deviation.** Dividing the sum of squared deviations by
`n` gives the spread of the numbers you have; dividing by `n − 1` estimates the spread of
the process that produced them. A benchmark reports the second, because the claim is about
the machine, not about those particular 1000 trials. At n = 1000 the difference is 0.05%,
but at n = 10 it is 5%, which is the size of the effect being measured. The engine uses
`n − 1` and says so in the header, the schema and the docs.

**Percentiles.** With 30 trials, where is the 95th percentile? Different tools give
different answers: nearest rank, lower, higher, midpoint, or linear interpolation. The
engine uses what numpy's default does: rank = p/100 × (n − 1), then linear interpolation
between the two neighbouring values. With four sorted values `[1, 2, 3, 4]`, the median is
rank 1.5, halfway between 2 and 3, so 2.5. Choosing a convention matters less than writing
it down and testing against a reference implementation, which is what the fixtures do.

**Coefficient of variation.** `stddev / mean`. It is dimensionless, so a CoV of 3% means
the same thing for ops/s, GB/s and nanoseconds. That is the whole reason the project's
variance target is expressed as a CoV rather than an absolute spread.

### Why Welford exists, and why the summaries do not use it

The obvious way to compute variance in one pass is to accumulate `Σx` and `Σx²` and use
`variance = (Σx² − n·mean²) / (n − 1)`. That is correct algebra and terrible arithmetic.
When the mean is large compared to the spread, `Σx²` and `n·mean²` are two huge, nearly
equal numbers, and subtracting them destroys the small difference you actually wanted.
This is called catastrophic cancellation.

Welford's algorithm avoids it by updating a running mean and a running sum of squared
deviations, so it never forms those two huge numbers. On the classic test case
`[1e9+1, 1e9+2, 1e9+3]` it returns a variance of exactly 1.0, while the naive formula
returns garbage. Welford also merges: two accumulators built from different halves can be
combined into the accumulator for the whole, which is what you need for per-thread or
streaming statistics.

So Welford is good. It is just not the *best* available when every value is already in
memory. The two-pass algorithm computes the mean first, then makes a second pass summing
`(x − mean)²`. That subtraction cancels the large common offset exactly, leaving values of
the right magnitude to square. It is what numpy does. Measured on the deliberately
ill-conditioned fixture (500 values near 1e9 with a standard deviation near 1):

| algorithm | relative error against numpy |
|---|---|
| naive `Σx² − n·mean²` | 2.8 × 10⁰ (wrong by 28×) |
| Welford, single pass | 1.4 × 10⁻⁸ |
| two-pass | 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ |

Welford is eight orders of magnitude better than naive and six worse than two-pass. Since
the runner already holds every trial value in memory (it needs them sorted for the
percentiles anyway), the second pass is free, so `summarize()` uses two-pass and `Welford`
stays for the streaming case. The header says which is which and why.

The governing quantity is the **condition number** κ = mean / stddev. When κ is small,
every method works. The fixture has κ ≈ 10⁹, which is what makes it a test. Real benchmark
data sits in between: 1000 trials of `cpu_int` at 4 Gops/s with 3% variation gives κ ≈ 33,
where even the naive formula would survive. The pathological case is in the fixture set on
purpose, so the library is right at the point where it matters rather than only where it
is easy.

### Why the merge test does not use a 1e-9 tolerance

`Welford.merge` reassociates floating-point arithmetic: adding a then b then merging is not
bit-identical to adding them in one sequence, because floating-point addition is not
associative. The two answers agree to within roughly κ · ε, where ε ≈ 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ is double
precision. For the ill-conditioned fixture that is about 2 × 10⁻⁷, and the measured
discrepancy was 4.6 × 10⁻⁸. So the test computes κ from the data and scales the tolerance
by it. For ordinary cases κ is single digits and the test is tight enough to catch a real
bug; for the pathological case it permits exactly what the arithmetic permits, and no more.
A flat 1e-9 would have been a test that fails for being right.

### Why MAD is reported next to stddev

The median absolute deviation is `median(|x − median(x)|)`: the typical distance from the
middle. A single catastrophic trial moves the standard deviation a lot and the MAD barely
at all. Reporting both turns a pair of numbers into a diagnostic, because for clean
Gaussian noise `stddev ≈ 1.4826 × MAD`, and a larger ratio means the tail is carrying the
variance. The thread sweep shows this directly:

| threads | stddev / MAD | reading |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.54 | close to Gaussian |
| 2 | 2.81 | a few slow trials dominate |
| 4 | 1.52 | close to Gaussian |
| 8 | 1.66 | close to Gaussian |

At 2 threads the slowest trial was 30% below the median. MAD does not remove that trial or
excuse it, it just makes visible that the spread is a tail and not a width. That distinction
decides what to do next: a wide, symmetric distribution means the measurement is noisy,
while a tight distribution with a tail means something occasionally interrupts it, which on
this machine is the host preempting a vCPU (see `M2.1.md`).

**MAD is a diagnostic, never a substitute.** The project's CoV target is computed from the
raw standard deviation with no trimming. For the record: dropping the three slowest of 30
trials would have taken the 8-thread CoV from 3.25% to 2.18%, which is exactly why that is
not allowed. Deciding which trials to discard after seeing the results is how a benchmark
starts reporting the number that was wanted rather than the number that was measured.

### Why the cap is checked at trial boundaries

`--max-seconds` could have been enforced with a timer that interrupts whatever is running.
It is not, for the same reason nothing else in the engine interrupts a trial: a partial
trial is a wrong measurement, not a short one. The deadline is checked at the top of the
trial loop, so the worst case is one trial length of overshoot (the run below asked for
3 seconds and took 3.53, with 50 ms trials plus fixed warmup). What the cap stops is the
*next* trial, and everything already measured is kept, summarized and written. The same
path serves Ctrl-C, which M2.1 built.

### Why summaries are validated, not just written

The summary is the first thing in this project that is a *derived* number rather than a
measured one, and derived numbers can be wrong in ways that are invisible. A wrong
`cov` still looks like a plausible float. So `bench validate` now checks summary entries
for the invariants that hold by construction: min ≤ median ≤ max, p5 ≤ p95, stddev and cov
and mad non-negative, n ≥ 1, and the metric matching its workload. These cannot catch a
subtly wrong mean, which is what the numpy fixtures are for, but they catch a summary
attached to the wrong data, which fixtures cannot.

## What the Verify steps showed

### 1. Against the numpy fixtures, tolerance 1e-9

`Stats.MatchesNumpyFixtures` passes over ten committed arrays, checking nine statistics
each (`mean`, `std`, `median`, `p5`, `p95`, `min`, `max`, `cov`, `mad`) plus agreement
between `summarize()` and the standalone functions:

```
two_values(n=2), small_odd(n=7), small_even(n=8), uniform_100(n=100),
lognormal_1000(n=1000), lognormal_1001(n=1001), large_offset_cancellation(n=500),
bimodal(n=600), heavy_tail(n=400), sorted_ascending(n=50)      [numpy 2.4.6, seed 20260918]
```

The set is chosen to cover what the definitions actually disagree about: odd and even `n`
(median interpolation), n = 2 (the smallest sample variance), a 1000-point log-normal
resembling real trial data, a heavy tail, a bimodal distribution, and the ill-conditioned
case. This tolerance is what forced the two-pass decision described above.

### 2. Welford on `[1e9+1, 1e9+2, 1e9+3]`

Variance exactly 1.0, mean exactly 1e9 + 2, asserted with `EXPECT_DOUBLE_EQ` rather than a
tolerance because the algorithm should be exact here.

### 3. Merging halves equals one pass

For every fixture case and four split points (0, n/3, n/2, n), a merged pair of
accumulators matches a single-pass accumulator in `n`, `mean` and `variance`, within the
condition-scaled tolerance. The split points include the degenerate ends, so merging with
an empty accumulator is covered in both directions.

### 4. End to end

A thread sweep produced one summary per configuration, and every field was recomputed
independently in Python from the emitted per-trial values:

| threads | n | mean Gops/s | median | stddev | CoV | MAD | min | p5 | p95 | max |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 30 | 3.839 | 3.885 | 0.431 | 11.24% | 0.280 | 2.403 | 3.070 | 4.303 | 4.319 |
| 2 | 30 | 7.718 | 8.132 | 0.821 | 10.63% | 0.292 | 5.729 | 6.032 | 8.447 | 8.510 |
| 4 | 30 | 16.107 | 16.258 | 1.096 | 6.80% | 0.722 | 12.912 | 13.848 | 17.256 | 17.297 |
| 8 | 30 | 28.540 | 28.784 | 0.928 | 3.25% | 0.561 | 26.250 | 26.523 | 29.431 | 29.602 |

Worst relative difference between the engine and the independent recomputation, across all
ten fields and all four configurations: **2.5 × 10⁻¹⁶**, which is one unit in the last
place of a double.

The CoV column is not the project's variance result. That target is 3% over 1000+ trials
with cold caches, and it belongs to M2.5; these are 30 warm trials and the numbers are what
they are. Worth noting anyway: variation *falls* as threads are added, from 11% to 3%. A
single-threaded trial is at the mercy of whichever vCPU it landed on, while an 8-thread
trial averages over eight of them, so the aggregate is steadier than any of its parts.

### 5. The safety cap

`bench run -t 1,2 -n 1000 --trial-ms 50 --max-seconds 3` asked for 2000 trials and stopped
after 50, in 3.53 seconds wall time:

```
bench: --max-seconds 3 reached; writing results collected so far (50 trials, 1 summaries)
```

The output validates, the second thread count never started, and the single summary has
`n = 50`, matching the 50 results exactly. Nothing about a truncated run is silently
different from a complete one except that there is less of it.

### 6. Test suites

55 functional tests pass in the release, debug, **asan** and **tsan** presets
(`ctest -LE perf`), and both perf-labelled tests still pass when run alone (false sharing
11.9×, spin barrier median start spread 0.11 µs).

Three test problems surfaced while doing this, all pre-existing and all fixed:

- The new tests were the first to call `validate_run` on a run built with a
  default-constructed `MachineInfo`, which has an empty `machine.id` and is correctly
  rejected. The tests now build a valid machine block. The summaries were never the
  problem; the failure message just made it look that way, which is a good reminder to
  read what a validator actually said.
- `Timing.ClockSelfTestPasses` asserted that a clock read costs under 100 ns. Under
  AddressSanitizer it costs 178 ns, because the sanitizer instruments every call. The
  assertion is now skipped under ASan and TSan with the measured value printed. The
  engine's own runtime warning fires there too, and that is correct behaviour: sanitizer
  builds are for finding bugs, not for measuring.
- `Runner.OpsScaleWithTrialLength` compared the work done in a 60 ms trial against a 30 ms
  trial and expected a ratio near 2.0. Because a trial runs *whole batches*, it overshoots
  by up to one batch, and under TSan a batch is a large fraction of a short trial, so the
  ratio reached 2.53. The test now compares the ops ratio against the measured *duration*
  ratio, which is the invariant that was always meant: the rate does not change with trial
  length. That is granularity-free and holds under any instrumentation.

## Five interview questions

**1. Your benchmark reports a standard deviation. Did you divide by n or n − 1, and does it
matter?**
n − 1, the sample standard deviation. The claim being made is about the machine, not about
those particular trials, so the spread of the underlying process is what is wanted and
dividing by n biases that low. At 1000 trials the difference is 0.05% and nobody would
notice; at 10 trials it is 5%, which is larger than the effects benchmarks are usually
trying to detect. Whichever is chosen, it has to be written down, because a reader
comparing two tools cannot tell from the number alone.

**2. Why not compute variance as `(Σx² − n·mean²) / (n − 1)`?**
It is correct algebra and numerically unusable when the mean is large relative to the
spread. Both terms become huge and nearly equal, so the subtraction cancels away the
answer; on values near 1e9 with a true standard deviation near 1, it was wrong by a factor
of 28 and can even return a negative variance. Welford's online algorithm avoids forming
those terms and gets within 1e-8; the two-pass algorithm, which computes the mean first and
then sums squared deviations, gets within 1e-14. Use two-pass when the data fits in memory
and Welford when it does not.

**3. What is a coefficient of variation and why report it instead of a standard deviation?**
CoV is the standard deviation divided by the mean, so it is dimensionless. It makes "how
repeatable is this measurement" comparable across metrics with different units and across
machines with different absolute speeds: 3% means the same thing for a 4 Gops/s CPU number
and a 90 ns latency number. The catch is that it is meaningless when the mean can be near
zero or can change sign, which is why it suits throughput and latency and would not suit,
say, a temperature delta.

**4. Your trial values have a standard deviation of 0.82 and a MAD of 0.29. What does that
tell you, and what should you do about it?**
For clean Gaussian noise the standard deviation is about 1.48 times the MAD. A ratio of 2.8
says the spread is coming from a tail, not from the width of the distribution: most trials
cluster tightly and a few are far off. That points at an intermittent external cause, such
as another process, a frequency change, or in a VM the host preempting a vCPU, rather than
at a noisy measurement. What to do is investigate and fix the cause, or report the tail
honestly. What not to do is delete the slow trials: on this data, dropping the slowest 10%
would have cut the reported CoV from 3.25% to 2.18%, and a number obtained that way is a
number chosen rather than measured.

**5. You add a `--max-seconds` cap to a long benchmark run. Where do you check it?**
At trial boundaries, never inside a trial. A trial interrupted halfway has measured a
partial amount of work over a full time window, which is not a short measurement but a
wrong one. Checking between trials costs at most one trial of overshoot and keeps every
completed result valid. The output should then be explicit that it was truncated, and any
summary should cover exactly the trials that ran, so nobody later reads a 50-trial summary
as if it were the 1000 trials that were requested.
